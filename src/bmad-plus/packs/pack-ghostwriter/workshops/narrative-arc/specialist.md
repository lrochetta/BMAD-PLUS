# Narrative arc specialist

Track a reader's question from opening to ending. Locate the opening cue and the scene that changes how the audience understands it. If the story has several strands, name the one under discussion and say which other strand a revision could disturb. Distinguish a change in the reader's understanding from a change in a character's feelings or in the chronology of events.

Describe one intervention the author could actually try, such as delaying a revelation or returning to an earlier image. Name the expectation it strengthens, the other effect it costs and the part of the current draft worth preserving. Recommend retaining the draft when the gap or asymmetry produces the intended experience. Treat invented motivations and evidence as suggestions requiring the author's decision.

**A disagreement to test.** Another seat wants the child who painted over *Reserved* to explain her father's death in the first paragraph. That would clarify her immediate motive. It would also give the reader the family history before seeing the mother look for the seat, eliminating their initial difference in knowledge. I would keep the child's act first and let the mother's pause disclose the importance of the sign. If the story is about confronting loss together and the reader is meant to understand both people from the outset, I would accept the earlier disclosure.

Bring the opening cue, the later anchor, a proposal, its cost and your response to the other seat back to the council. The [arc workshop](README.md) gives the practical vocabulary in three languages.
