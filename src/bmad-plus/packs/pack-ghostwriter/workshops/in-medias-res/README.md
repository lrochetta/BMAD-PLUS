# In medias res

**Commencer dans le vif de l’action · Begin in the midst of the action · Começar no meio da ação.** Use this optional reading when an author wants to test where the reader should enter a story. The technique concerns the order in which the reader encounters events. The opening may be as quiet as a name being crossed off a guest list. In the *Ars poetica*, Horace praises Homer for bringing the listener into events already under way. [Horace, *Ars poetica*, lines 146–152](https://adfonteslibrary.org/work-horace-ars-poetica)

## Test the opening

Choose two starting points from the actual manuscript. Under each, write what the reader can tell by the end of the first paragraph. Name the person in focus, what is happening now and which immediate question is worth following. Then find the first later action the reader could misread without an earlier fact. Place that fact at or before this point. Use evidence already present in the story. A gesture, a line of dialogue or the order of two existing scenes may do the work. Preserve the chronology of the events themselves unless the author also asked to change the plot.

## An opening at the clerk’s desk

In a fictional scene, Isa waits in the queue to register her infant. The draft explains the registration rule, describes the wait, then reaches the line “The clerk’s stamp hovered over the empty box.” The next paragraph says that Isa can enter either parent’s name and has promised her sister she will enter her own. The blank itself can hold the reader’s interest. The promise must reach the reader before Isa turns the form face down, or the gesture could read as simple shyness.

Suppose a colleague recommends opening with the full explanation of the rule. That gives the reader immediate certainty. The counterproposal is to open on the hovering stamp and move the existing sentence about the promise to the moment before Isa turns the form. Try the new order aloud, keeping the clerk’s words unchanged. The author can hear whether the silence now feels charged or merely confusing. In the latter case, keep the earlier orientation.
