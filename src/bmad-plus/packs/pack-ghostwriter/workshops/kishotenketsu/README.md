# Kishōtenketsu · 起承転結

Use this workshop when an author wants a later image, scene or change of viewpoint to alter the reader’s understanding of an earlier passage. Give the [specialist](specialist.md) the passage, any neighbouring scenes supplied by the author and the intended effect of the ending.

## Four movements

| Movement | English · français · português brasileiro | Question for the manuscript |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 起 · ki | Introduction · introduction · introdução | Which person, object or situation does the reader first notice? |
| 承 · shō | Development · développement · desenvolvimento | Which new detail develops that opening and leads the reader to expect something? |
| 転 · ten | Turn · tournant · virada | What changes when the reader sees this detail from another position, at another time or beside another image? |
| 結 · ketsu | Conclusion · conclusion · desfecho | Where can the reader see the connection between the opening and the turn? What can the ending leave implicit? |

The name and readings above are Japanese. This arrangement has historical roots in Chinese composition and has acquired different uses across genres. In a study of Japanese newspaper prose, Yoshifumi Takahashi describes how a new perspective in *ten* can supply a way to answer the opening question. Ryuko Kubota documents differences among Japanese writing teachers’ interpretations and cautions against generalizing from a few examples to an entire culture. [Takahashi, Hokkaido University](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/38486/) · [Kubota, Written Communication](https://doi.org/10.1177/0741088397014004002)

For a concrete manga application, Shogakukan’s lesson uses the arrival of a transfer student’s former girlfriend as its turn. Rivalry fits that particular story. The lesson also describes varying the space each movement receives. In another manuscript a quiet change of viewpoint may do the work. Keep or introduce conflict according to the author’s material and intent. [Shogakukan’s manga workshop](https://shincomi.shogakukan.co.jp/training/015.html)

## An ending for the print shop

**Invented scene.** At opening time, a printmaker pulls an uneven impression of a wren and lays it face down. By winter his drawer holds a flock of crooked birds. Across town, a bookbinder smooths a speckled sheet against the inside cover of a sketchbook. Her daughter opens the book, writes her name under the wren and closes it.

The drawer develops the sense of accumulation. The cut across town changes what the reader thinks the sheets are for. The bird under the girl’s name connects the opening image with its second life. One available revision is to leave a distinctive smudge on the wren’s wing in both scenes if readers miss that connection.

A Three Acts specialist might propose an earlier request from the bookbinder so the reader anticipates a delivery. That would give the printmaker a task and establish the destination. It would also reveal the connection before the book opens. If the author wants suspense about getting the paper there, try the request. If recognition on the last image matters more, try the repeated smudge. Read both endings aloud with the author’s stated aim in view.

Delegate the [specialist card](specialist.md) for a council reading. Keep this exercise optional for factual prose. Where readers need an instruction or an emergency decision immediately, lead with that information.
