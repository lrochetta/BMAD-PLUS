# The Snowflake Method

**La méthode du flocon de neige · The Snowflake Method · Método do floco de neve.** Randy Ingermanson describes an iterative process for designing a novel. He expands a compact premise into a synopsis, develops the people living through the events, revisits earlier decisions as their motives become clearer and derives a list of scenes. For this workshop, choose the smallest expansion that can answer the author's current question. A comedy and a tragedy can use the same planning process and produce very different plots. [Randy Ingermanson’s original guide](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/)

## Try one expansion

State the current premise in one sentence. Choose a consequential action in the synopsis and retell it briefly from the perspective of the person who must perform it. What do they stand to lose? What do they expect the action will accomplish? Find the scene where the reader can see them discover, decide or act. If the scene answers differently from the synopsis, bring the discrepancy back to the premise. The author can change either level or keep the uncertainty for drafting. One affected scene is sufficient for a local question.

## A missing choice in a synopsis

A fictional synopsis reads, “A librarian returns a borrowed telescope before the village auction begins.” The last scene shows the village's baker bidding on the same telescope. The middle says only, “The telescope changes hands.” Neither the synopsis nor the scene tells us who chose to put it up for sale. Several readings remain possible, including an accidental sale and a deliberate offer from the owner. Each would give the baker's bid a different meaning.

Suppose another specialist suggests adding an angry confrontation at the auction. It would give the characters a public moment of decision. The Snowflake counterproposal is to expand “The telescope changes hands” into a scene card from the owner's viewpoint before drafting more dialogue. The card should identify who decides to sell and what they hope the bid will secure. If the author wants the reader never to know who authorized the sale, keep that answer private in the planning notes. This exercise may save a later rewrite, but settling the answer now can also narrow a fruitful ambiguity.
